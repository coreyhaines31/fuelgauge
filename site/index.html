Forty files into a refactor. Then: limit reached.

Claude Code is halfway through moving your tests to Vitest. Then the replies stop. You've hit your 5-hour limit, and it resets at 5:00. The branch doesn't build, half the files are on the new runner, and you've got nearly three hours to wait.

You checked your usage that morning. It said 55%, and 55% sounded fine. What it didn't say was that at the pace you were going, 55% at 11 a.m. meant out by 2.

“Can I start this now?”

Before the big job, you want to know if the window will last, not how full it is.

“Should I switch to Codex?”

If one tool is running short and the other isn't, the next task should go to the one that makes it.

“Will the weekly limit last until Thursday?”

The weekly limit is the one that ends a week early, and you only find out when it does.

55% used. Is that a problem?

It depends on how fast you're going and how long until the reset. Fuel Gauge does that math for every limit and gives you the answer in plain words: “Makes it to reset” or “Runs out around 11:50a.”

How the forecast works.

Read, measure, project. It runs every time a new reading arrives, entirely on your Mac.

1

It reads what's already there

A reading every time Claude Code replies, through its status line. Codex's session logs and Grok Bot's local usage cache fill in the rest.

2

It measures your pace

How much usage climbed over the recent stretch: the last hour for 5-hour limits, the last day for weekly ones.

3

It projects to the reset

If that pace holds, do you hit 100% first? You get a verdict and a range, not a fake-precise minute.

It waits for enough readings before it forecasts: 10 minutes for a 5-hour limit, 3 hours for a weekly one. Until then it says “Learning your pace.” It's an estimate from your recent pace, not a promise.

It learns your week.

Weekly and monthly limits depend on when you work, not just how fast. After two weeks of readings, Fuel Gauge learns your usual hours, separately for weekdays and weekends. A forecast made on Friday night doesn't assume you'll code all weekend at Friday's pace.

The last three days nudge that pattern up or down, by up to 2×. A busy session counts for a few hours, then fades. Until there's enough history, it uses your recent pace. Hours it hasn't seen aren't assumed to be quiet; it falls back to your recent pace instead.

Forecast details shows exactly what it learned: weekday and weekend bars by hour, the readings behind each one, and how this week compares with usual.

It checks its own forecasts.

Every hour, while the app is open, Fuel Gauge freezes its forecast for each limit: where you'll be in 1, 6, and 24 hours. When the real reading arrives, it scores the guess. You don't have to take the forecast on faith.

  • Predicted vs. actual plots saved predictions as diamonds and real readings as circles, with the gap between them. Pick 1, 6, or 24 hours ahead, over the last 7 or 30 days.
  • It shows the average error in percentage points for its own forecast and for the plain recent-pace method, on the same outcomes. A score appears once there are 20 comparisons across three days.
  • A missed reading stays a gap. It's never counted as zero usage.
  • fuelgauge backtest --days 7 replays your saved history to compare the two methods. Local and read-only.

The meter is the whole story at a glance: needle for fuel left, orange for running short, red for out. Next to it, show nothing, the percent used, or the time until you run out. Hover it for the most urgent limit's verdict.

You get one alert per window, and only when two fresh readings agree you'll run out at least 30 minutes early. Alerts at 80% and 95% used are on by default; 50% is there if you want it. macOS asks once, at the first alert, whether Fuel Gauge may send notifications.

Know when you have a reset to spare.

When a limit runs out, a saved reset can get you going again. Fuel Gauge shows what you have, and never spends one for you.

Codex

The number of resets you can redeem and when the next one expires, checked every five minutes through your installed, signed-in Codex CLI. Redeem in Codex's /usage menu, then hit Refresh. On by default; turn it off in Settings › Resets.

Claude

Claude's status line doesn't include this, so save the count by hand, with an optional expiry. The card labels it as manual and shows when you recorded it.

Forecasts stay honest

A forecast never assumes you'll redeem a reset. If you're running short with two resets in hand, it still says you're running short.

Where the built-ins win

They're official and already installed. Claude Code's /usage also breaks down what's using your plan, like skills, subagents, and MCP servers, with suggestions. The usage page on claude.ai covers every device.

Where the menu bar trackers win

Breadth. If you use Gemini, Copilot, Z.ai, or a dozen other services, they cover them in one menu, and both already forecast when you'll run out. CodexBar also has a big community and a Linux CLI.

Where ccstatusline wins

If all you want is a status line, it's a deep one: forecast and limit-timer widgets alongside dozens of others, right where you type.

Also worth knowing: Claude Usage Tracker and Usage4Claude show Claude and Codex percentages and reset times in the menu bar, with pace markers, using a pasted session key or a sign-in. ccusage reports tokens and cost across about 20 agent CLIs. Fuel Gauge's case is narrower: the verdict first, a forecast that knows your week and checks itself, an exit code your agents can use, and no browser cookies or pasted session keys.

Your usage stays on your Mac.

Exactly what it reads, what it saves, and the few times it goes online.

Local files only

Claude Code's status line input and logs, Codex's logs in ~/.codex, and Grok Bot's usage cache. No browser cookies, no keychain, and it never asks you to sign in. The few things that go online are listed under Zero telemetry.

No Full Disk Access

It doesn't need it. Everything it reads sits in your home folder. The only permission it asks for is notifications, at the first alert.

Zero telemetry

No analytics, nothing about your usage sent anywhere. It goes online for update checks, Codex reset counts through your Codex CLI and Claude’s Fable allowance through Claude Code (on by default, off in Settings › Resets), and Cursor if you turn it on.

One alert, early

Once per window, when the forecast says you'll run out at least 30 minutes early. Alerts at 80% and 95% used are on by default, 50% is there if you want it, and any of them can be turned off.

Questions

Is it accurate?

It's an estimate from your recent pace, not a promise. Speed up or slow down and the forecast moves with you. That's why it shows a range, like “Between 11:31a and 12:14p at your recent pace,” instead of one exact minute, and why it waits for enough readings before it forecasts at all: 10 minutes for a 5-hour limit, 3 hours for a weekly one.

Does it work with Claude Pro, Max, and Ultra?

Yes. It uses the 5-hour and weekly limits Claude Code reports for your plan, so it works on Pro, Max, and Ultra. The status line is where those readings come from, so turn it on.

Which tools does it track?

Claude Code's 5-hour and weekly limits, Codex's limits (5-hour and weekly, or weekly only on plans without a 5-hour limit), Grok Bot's weekly limit, and, if you turn it on, Cursor's monthly usage. Choose which ones to watch in Settings › Tools.

How is it different from Claude Code's /usage?

/usage tells you where you are: how much of each limit you've used and when it resets, plus what's using it. Fuel Gauge tells you where you'll be at the reset, at your pace, without you asking. It also covers Codex and Grok Bot, and puts the answer in your menu bar and under your prompt.

Does it send my data anywhere?

No. Your usage stays on your Mac. No browser cookies, no Full Disk Access, no keychain, no sign-in, and zero telemetry. It goes online only to check for updates, to count Codex resets through your installed Codex CLI and read Claude’s Fable allowance through Claude Code (turn those off in Settings › Resets), and for Cursor if you turn Cursor on.

How is it different from CodexBar?

CodexBar covers far more providers, over 100, and also shows when you'll run out. Fuel Gauge covers four tools and leads with the verdict: will you make it to reset. It learns your weekly pattern, scores its own forecasts, adds a Claude Code status line and an exit code agents can check, and never reads browser cookies. The full comparison.

Will it break my existing status line?

No. If you already have one, it keeps showing, with the forecast on a line under it. Your settings.json is backed up first, and fuelgauge uninstall-statusline puts everything back.

Will it slow down my Mac or drain the battery?

It checks once a minute. After the first import of your usage history, it reads only new log records and keeps a local cache. The status line runs once after each Claude Code reply: it saves one reading and prints one line.

How do I remove it?

Run fuelgauge uninstall-statusline first, so your Claude Code status line goes back to how it was. Then run brew uninstall --cask --zap fuelgauge, which also removes its saved readings. If you installed from the DMG, drag Fuel Gauge from Applications to the Trash, and trash ~/Library/Application Support/Fuel Gauge.

How do updates work?

The app checks for new versions with Sparkle and installs them when you say so. Turn automatic checks off in Settings › General. If you installed with Homebrew, brew upgrade --cask fuelgauge works too.

Is it really free?

Yes, at home or at work. No account, no trial, no subscription. The source is on GitHub under the FSL-1.1-MIT license.

Which Macs does it run on?

Any Mac with macOS 14 Sonoma or later, Apple Silicon or Intel.
